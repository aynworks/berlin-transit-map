Design rationale
Four Decisions.
One System.

Not four aesthetic choices. Four positions taken after reading the city — its geography, its history, its daily users. Each one traceable.

01
New 60° Grid
Grid system: departure from 1992

The existing Berlin map has used a 45° grid since 1992. Four axes. The system works technically. But it produces layouts that feel mechanical, almost rigid, with angular transitions that don't correspond to how the city actually bends.

The 60° tri-axial system changes this. Three axes instead of four. Inspired by Constantine Konovalov's work on the Moscow 2030 transit vision adapted here for Berlin's specific geographic logic. The grid follows the city, not the other way around.

02
Ring Bahn
Restoring the Dog's Head

Berlin's Ringbahn wraps the city center in a distinctive shape — locals call it the Hundekopf, the dog's head. It's a mental model, a way to orient yourself in the city. The current map abstracts it away into geometric cleanliness.

This design brings it back. Not literally the grid still holds but legibly enough that the shape reads. Geography and geometry working together, not one erasing the other.

03
Geographically Bridged
Real landmarks, clear reference

Transit maps typically flatten geography to pure topology. Connections matter, distances don't. That works until it doesn't — when a tourist needs to know which side of the Spree they're on, or whether Tiergarten is walkable from their stop.

This map bridges both. The grid keeps it schematic, but the river stays visible, parks are marked, districts are legible. Just enough geography to ground the network in the city's actual form.

Shape to context

The 2021 format was a production default, inherited and unchallenged. The new 60° tri-axial grid that structures the lines also determines the outer frame. East and west, re-uniting the city's historical fracture. The pointed ends push beyond the frame's boundary. Berlin has always extended past its own edges.

Inside carriages, the hexagonal form fits the door panels, keeping passengers oriented along the east-west axis in transit. On station platforms, the rectangle fits the standardised "Reclame" panel slots of Berlin's historic stations — more surface area, readable at distance.

04
Highlighted Transfers
Critical junctions, clear hierarchy

On a transit map, transfers are the hardest thing to get right. They need to show connection without adding visual noise. Berlin's maps originally marked every station the same way: a square, a dot, a bar. Transfer or terminus, the symbol was identical.

By 1992, the system introduced the capsule shape — an outlined form grouping stations at a shared transfer point. A step forward in legibility, but the capsule stayed empty. It told passengers that lines connected. Not which ones.

That gap matters. At a busy hub, knowing a transfer exists is not enough. The new capsule carries that information directly. Line indicators sit inside the outline, high contrast, immediately scannable. The boundary groups. The content informs. Both read in under a second.

Berlin Transit map is my vision of a more efficient city. By creating this map, we want to launch a discussion about future graphic solutions for the transit identity of Berlin. The idea of the new Berlin Transit map came into picture when I travel on the network, not seeing all the transport being visible and having to use Google Maps at all times during my ride because of the lack of mapping information.
